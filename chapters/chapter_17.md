[H2]Chapter 17 — "Clean Hands"[/H2]

[CENTER][I]286 AC — Highgarden[/I][/CENTER]

The child's name was Bessa, and she was dying.

Or she had been dying, two days ago, when her mother brought her to the kitchen door of Highgarden with the blank, hollowed expression of a woman who'd already started mourning. The girl was four, maybe five; her mother wasn't certain of the year, which told you everything about the state of record-keeping among the smallfolk, and she'd been burning with fever for a week.

The local healer had prescribed a poultice of moss and river clay, applied to the chest. The septa had prescribed prayer. Both prescriptions had the same therapeutic value, which is to say none whatsoever.

I'd prescribed clean water, rest, willow bark tea for the fever, and washing. That last item was the part that made everyone stare. Hands washed before touching the child. The child's bedding washed in boiling water. The wound on her leg (a small cut, infected, almost certainly the source of the fever) cleaned with boiled water and diluted spirit, then wrapped in clean linen.

Not a poultice. Not a prayer. Soap and hot water and alcohol.

Maester Lomys had watched me give these instructions with the expression of a man being told that the sun rose in the west.

"The miasma—" he began.

"Is not relevant here," I said. "The wound has festered. The rot is what causes the fever. Clean the wound, the rot clears, the fever breaks. Miasma theory speaks of corruption carried on bad airs, and this is not carried on any air. It's a dirty cut that was packed with river clay, which is the worst possible thing you can put on an open wound short of actual manure."

"River clay has been used in healing for—"

"Centuries. Yes. And children have been dying of infected wounds for centuries. Perhaps the two facts are related."

That was sharper than I usually allowed myself to be. I was tired. I'd been up since dawn at the still-house, walking the crew Grandmother had vetted through the heads-and-tails cut on a fresh run of medical spirits, and the sight of this child shaking with fever because someone had packed mud into a wound had cracked something open in me that I normally kept sealed.

You couldn't save everyone. I knew that. I'd known it since the fever that killed whatever child had inhabited this body before me, the fever that had brought me here with a head full of knowledge and no way to deploy it fast enough. You couldn't save everyone, but you could save this one, if people would just listen.

Lomys had listened. Reluctantly, with visible skepticism, but he'd listened. Partly because I was the heir to Highgarden and he served at our pleasure. Partly because, and I could see this in his eyes, behind the professional doubt, he wanted me to be right. He'd watched children die of fevers his remedies couldn't touch. If washing and boiled water could do what poultices couldn't, he wanted to know.

That was the difference between Lomys and the worst of the Citadel. He was conservative, not closed. Skeptical, not hostile. He'd resist new ideas, but he'd yield to new evidence.

I just had to give him the evidence.

Bessa's fever broke on the third day.

Her mother wept. The kitchen staff, who'd been keeping quiet vigil in the way that servants do when a child's life is at stake, exhaled collectively. Even Lomys allowed himself a small, cautious nod.

"One case proves nothing," he said.

He was right. One case proved nothing. That was sound scientific thinking, and I respected him for it.

"Then we'll need more cases," I said.

I didn't have to wait long.

Life in a medieval castle, even a prosperous one, generated a steady stream of minor injuries and illnesses. Kitchen burns. Training-yard cuts. Seasonal fevers. Stomach ailments from water that was, by any modern standard, a biohazard in liquid form.

I'd been quietly improving Highgarden's practices for over a year, but quietly was the operative word. A suggestion here, a request there: could the drinking water be boiled first? Could the kitchen staff wash their hands before preparing food? Could wound dressings be boiled before application? Small changes, introduced without fanfare, attributed to books I'd supposedly read or advice from Mother's Hightower connections.

Now I pushed harder.

I had the soap. This was one of my quieter innovations.

Westeros already had soap. The washerwomen made it the way it had been made for a thousand years: boil rendered tallow in lye leached from wood ash, stir until the mixture thickened, ladle the result into wooden molds. The bars that came out the other end were brown, brittle, faintly redolent of the animal that had supplied the fat, and stripped the top layer of skin off anyone who used them daily. They cleaned, in the technical sense that they removed dirt. They did it by chemically dissolving everything they touched, including you.

The reason was simple. The washerwomen had no reliable way to measure the strength of their lye. Wood ash was a variable input: beech leached differently than oak, fresh ash differently than weathered, and the same hopper gave different lye depending on how long the water sat in it. So the makers erred on the strong side, because under-leached lye produced soft, useless soap and over-leached lye at least produced soap that worked. The result was a product that worked at the cost of the hands using it.

What I had done was, in The Book's terms, almost embarrassingly simple. First, standardize the lye. A fresh egg floats with about a copper-piece-sized circle of shell above the surface in lye of the right strength, sinks in weak lye, and rides up high in lye that will burn the bar. Three ash-leachings to find the concentration, then a single test before each batch. Second, teach the boil to grain. A measured pour of brine at the right point in the cook separated the soap from the glycerin water and the excess lye, leaving a harder, milder bar that was actually neutral to the skin. Third, cure the bars on a slatted shelf for six weeks before use, so the saponification reaction had time to finish. Soap straight out of the mold was still chemically active. Soap that had cured was a tool. The washerwomen used it the day it came out of the mold, and that alone explained half the harshness.

My version was smoother, lathered better, and didn't strip the skin raw. It had taken months of experimentation, most of it conducted in the workshop with Tobho rolling his eyes and asking if he was a blacksmith or a washerwoman's apprentice.

"You're whatever I need you to be," I'd told him, and he'd laughed, because Tobho had learned that my strange projects usually led somewhere worthwhile.

The soap was the foundation. You couldn't implement a handwashing protocol without effective soap; it was like trying to build a house without nails. Now I had nails.

I started with the kitchen.

"Before preparing any food," I told Eda, the head cook, "everyone washes their hands. With soap. Properly, between the fingers, under the nails. Every time."

Eda was a large woman with forearms like hams and a will to match. She'd fed Highgarden's household for fifteen years, and she did not appreciate being told how to run her kitchen by a child.

"My lord," she said, in the careful tone reserved for people you disagree with who can have you dismissed, "we've always kept a clean kitchen."

"I know you have. This isn't about cleanliness, it's about protection. There are things on our hands we can't see that cause sickness." I caught myself. Careful. I couldn't explain bacteria. I didn't have a microscope, couldn't prove their existence, couldn't show anyone the invisible world of pathogens that was, right now, colonizing every surface in this kitchen. "The Seven showed me," I said.

It was the first time I'd deployed the cover story in earnest. The words felt strange in my mouth, manipulative, dishonest, exploitative of people's genuine faith. I didn't enjoy it.

But I'd enjoy dead children even less.

"During the fever," I continued. "I saw — it was like a vision. Tiny things, too small to see, living on our hands, in our water. They cause disease. Not bad air, not evil humors. These tiny things. And they can be killed by soap, by boiling water, by the spirits we distill. The Seven showed me how to fight them."

Eda's expression changed. The skepticism didn't vanish, she was too practical a woman for that, but something else appeared alongside it. Faith. Not in me, particularly, but in the possibility that the Seven might speak to a child who'd nearly died. The Reach was devout. Not fanatically so, but the Faith of the Seven was woven into daily life here, and divine visions were an accepted, if rare, phenomenon.

"You saw this in the fever," she said.

"I saw many things in the fever." That, at least, was true. I'd seen an entire world: hospitals and microscopes and antibiotics, public health campaigns and germ theory and clean water standards. I'd seen everything that was possible and everything that I couldn't yet prove. "The handwashing works, Eda. I've tested it. Bessa, the girl with the fever, she's alive because we cleaned her wound properly instead of packing it with clay."

"I heard about the girl."

Word traveled fast in a castle. Faster than disease, ironically.

"Will you try it? A month. If the kitchen staff wash properly before every meal preparation, and the illness rate doesn't drop, I'll never mention it again."

She agreed. Grudgingly, but she agreed.

The next week was a campaign. Not in the military sense; I was ten years old and the most dangerous thing I'd ever wielded was a pair of tongs. A campaign of persuasion, demonstration, and relentless, systematic evidence-gathering.

I established protocols. I hated the word even as I used it; it sounded too modern, too clinical, too much like the world I'd come from, but there was no better term for what I was doing. Structured procedures, consistently applied, with documented outcomes.

The kitchen protocol: handwashing before food preparation. Boiled water for drinking. All surfaces cleaned daily with diluted vinegar, another of my quiet innovations, the recognition that acid inhibited bacterial growth, even if I couldn't explain why in terms anyone here would accept.

The wound protocol: clean the wound with boiled water. Apply diluted spirit, specifically Reach Spirit, which had a high enough alcohol content to serve as antiseptic. Wrap in boiled linen. Change the dressing daily. Wash hands before and after treating the wound.

The sick-room protocol: isolate the patient. Boil all bedding and clothing. Limit visitors. Everyone who enters washes hands on the way in and the way out.

I wrote these protocols down. Simple language, clear instructions, no theory. I didn't explain why these things worked (I couldn't, not without a century of science I couldn't reproduce), only that they did.

And then I tracked the results.

I kept a ledger. A private one, hidden in my workshop alongside my other records. Every illness. Every injury. Every treatment. Every outcome. Date, patient, condition, treatment given, result.

The first month's data was small. A kitchen maid who cut her hand, cleaned and dressed properly, healed in a week with no infection. A stable boy with a stomach ailment, switched to boiled water, recovered in two days. Two children with fevers, willow bark tea and clean bedding, both recovered.

Small numbers. Anecdotal evidence. Not proof.

But the absence was as telling as the presence. In a typical month, Lomys told me, the household saw three to five cases of serious illness requiring bed rest. In the month after I implemented the kitchen protocols, there was one. A serving girl who'd been visiting family in the village, where the water wasn't boiled.

"Coincidence," Lomys said, but he said it quietly, and he was writing in his own ledger.

The second month was better. Six injuries treated with the wound protocol, all healed cleanly. Two cases of stomach illness, both in new arrivals who hadn't yet adapted to the boiled-water regimen. No fevers in the household at all.

The third month was when word began to spread.

It started with the servants, as most real changes do. The nobility could pass laws and issue decrees, but the people who actually ran a castle (the cooks, the washerwomen, the stable hands, the gardeners) were the ones whose daily practices determined whether everyone lived or died.

And they talked.

"The young lord's water," they called the boiled water. "The young lord's soap." "The young lord's way of cleaning cuts." It spread from the kitchen to the laundry to the stables, carried by the invisible network of servant gossip that moved through Highgarden faster than any raven.

A washerwoman named Mira asked Eda. Mira's daughter had a cough that wouldn't clear; the village healer had prescribed steam and prayer; the cough had not cleared. Mira had stood at the kitchen door for the better part of a morning, polite at first, less polite as the morning wore on, and Eda, who six weeks ago would have sent her home with a clout to the ear for raising her voice in the kitchen, instead came and found me in the workshop.

"The washerwoman's girl is sick, my lord," Eda said. "I told her I'd ask."

Word traveled in both directions, then. Six weeks ago Eda had been a sceptic the protocols had to win over. Now she was a relay, and a brave one — for a head cook to walk a smallfolk request up to the heir was not a small thing in this household. I had not asked her to do it. She had decided.

"Tell Mira to bring her to Maester Lomys," I said, careful, because I was not a doctor and making medical diagnoses as a ten-year-old lord was a line I preferred not to cross too visibly. "Tell him I asked him to use the new protocols."

Lomys examined the girl. The cough was productive, a chest infection, probably bacterial, which in this world meant a coin flip between recovery and death.

"Clean water," I said, standing beside him in the sick room. "Willow bark for the fever. Honey and lemon for the throat. Honey has properties that fight infection, even if we don't fully understand why. Rest. Isolation. Clean bedding."

"And the spirit?"

"Not internally — she's too young, and the spirit is too strong for a child's stomach. But we clean everything that touches her with it. Hands, instruments, bedding."

Lomys did as I asked. The girl recovered in five days.

Then a gardener's boy, kicked by a horse. The wound was deep, ugly, the kind that usually went septic. I cleaned it myself — boiled water, careful irrigation to remove debris, spirit wash, clean dressing. Changed the dressing every day. Washed my hands before and after until they were red and raw.

The wound healed clean.

Then a cluster of stomach illness in two of the villages on the Tyrell home estate. Brightwater and Rookford, eight miles apart, similar in size and water source: the same shallow run of the Mander, separated by a low ridge. I sent the same instructions to both: boil all drinking water; wash hands before food; isolate the sick; burn the privies and dig fresh; soap supplied. A rider went out the same morning.

The rider reached Brightwater by midday. He reached Rookford two and a half days later, because a wheel split at the Rookford ford and a cart had to be sent the long way around. By the time the rider arrived, Rookford had buried thirteen.

Brightwater lost seven before the outbreak ran its course. Five of them children under three; two of them old. The rule of boiled water and washed hands could not save the worst-exposed, and there were always a handful of households that would not or could not keep a fire burning all day for water that already ran clear from the well. The pattern was not a wall. It was a filter, and the filter only worked on what passed through it in time.

Rookford lost thirty-eight before the curve turned, and a handful more in the slow tail of the outbreak as the last sceptical houses came around.

That was the turning point.

It wasn't scientifically rigorous; not by any standard I'd consider valid in my old life. Two villages was not a controlled trial. But the pattern was visible to anyone with eyes. Same illness, same water, same season. Different timing. Different death toll. The young lord's methods worked. Not sometimes, not mysteriously, not through divine caprice. Consistently. Predictably. Demonstrably.

And because I'd given them a story they could believe.

"The Seven showed you," Lomys said one evening. It wasn't a question.

We were in his study, surrounded by the tools of his trade: herbs, surgical instruments, the thick volumes of the Citadel's standard medical texts. I'd come to review the month's health records, which Lomys was now keeping meticulously, having recognized that data was more persuasive than argument.

"During the fever," I said. Consistent story. Never waver, never embellish, never add details that could be contradicted later.

"The Seven showed you about invisible creatures that cause disease."

"Yes."

He was quiet for a long moment. I could see him wrestling with it: the trained skepticism of a Citadel-educated maester versus the evidence of his own records, the recovering patients, the clean-healing wounds that should have gone septic.

"The Citadel teaches that disease arises from imbalanced humors and corrupted air," he said slowly. "This has been the foundation of medical practice for centuries."

"The Citadel also teaches that one should observe, record, and follow the evidence wherever it leads."

"In theory."

"Then let's test the theory. Your records show wound rot reduced by close to half since we began the cleaning protocols. Cases of the flux down by a third against the same season last year. You've documented this yourself."

"I have."

"Either the Seven gave me accurate guidance, or I happened to guess a set of practices that coincidentally produce better outcomes across every category of illness and injury. Which explanation requires fewer assumptions?"

Lomys looked at me over his half-moon spectacles. "You argue like a maester."

"I read a lot."

His lips twitched. Almost a smile. "The practices work. I cannot deny that. But I cannot explain why they work within the framework I was trained in, and that... troubles me."

"Would it help to know that Archmaester Ryam in Oldtown has been arguing for contact-based disease transmission for years? That there's a historical precedent, Archmaester Quillion, two centuries ago, who proposed that diseases travel on surfaces and in water rather than through air?"

The name caught his attention. "Quillion. I've heard the name. His work was discredited."

"His work was buried. There's a difference. Discredited means tested and found wanting. Buried means locked in the restricted archives because it contradicted established doctrine. Archmaester Ryam wrote me as much last month. He has agreed to send what he can copy of Quillion's [I]On Contagion[/I] under his own seal."

This was a risk. Criticizing the Citadel to a Citadel-trained maester was like criticizing the Faith to a septon; it could go either way. But I'd taken Lomys's measure over the past year, and I believed he was honest enough to hear it.

He was.

"You may be right," he said. "The Citadel is not infallible. But it is careful, and caution has its virtues."

"It does. But caution that prevents the adoption of life-saving practices isn't careful. It's negligent."

Lomys folded his hands on the desk. "You assume too much, my lord, about what the Citadel does not know. The midwives of the Mander villages have been boiling birthing cloths since before I took my chain. They call the practice scalding. It is in none of the texts I was taught from. I learned it from one of them, twenty years ago. I could not have told you why it worked, only that it did. And before you decide that we are wholly behind you, consider that pieces of the answer are already alive in this country. They live in the hands of women who do not write books."

I sat with that. I had assumed, as I usually assumed, that the gap between The Book's knowledge and Westerosi practice was uniform. It wasn't. There were villages of grandmothers practicing fragments of the right answer because trial had arrived where theory had not.

"I hadn't known," I said.

"You wouldn't have. Wet nurses do not publish."

Another long silence. Then he nodded. "I will continue documenting the outcomes. And I will write to the Citadel, to Archmaester Ryam since you mention him, with our findings."

"That's all I ask."

It wasn't all I asked. It was the beginning of what I asked. But one step at a time.

The divine vision story spread as fast as the practical results. Faster, maybe, because stories always traveled faster than evidence.

The young lord had nearly died of a fever, years ago. Everyone knew that. During the fever, the Seven had shown him things, secrets of healing, ways to fight disease that no maester had ever taught. He'd come back changed. Brilliant. Strange. Touched by the gods.

I hadn't invented this story. Not exactly. Grandmother had suggested it, early on, as a plausible explanation for my impossible knowledge. "Children don't read metallurgy texts," she'd said. "But children do see visions during deathbed fevers, and nobody can disprove a religious experience." It was cynical, pragmatic, and entirely correct.

But now, hearing it repeated by servants and smallfolk, watching it transform from a convenient excuse into something approaching genuine belief, I felt the weight of it.

These people trusted me because they thought the gods had spoken to me. That trust was saving lives: Bessa's life, the gardener's boy's life, the lives of everyone who drank boiled water instead of river water teeming with things that would kill them. The lie was doing good.

But it was still a lie.

I sat in my workshop that evening, surrounded by my tools and my ledgers and the organized chaos of a life spent trying to drag a medieval world toward something better, and I thought about the ethics of benevolent deception.

I didn't reach a conclusion. I suspected I never would. Some moral calculations didn't resolve neatly; they just sat in your chest like a stone, a permanent reminder that you were building something wonderful on a foundation of necessary dishonesty.

The alternative was truth, and the truth was impossible. [I]I'm a time-traveler from another world. I know about germs because my old civilization discovered them centuries from now. Everything I've taught you comes from science you won't develop for generations, and the Seven have nothing to do with it.[/I]

They'd lock me in a tower. Or worse — they'd believe me, and the questions that followed would be even more dangerous than the lie.

So the lie stood. And the children lived.

I could work with that.

The next morning, I went to see Lomys again. Not about the sanitation protocols, which were running themselves now, carried by the momentum of visible results and servant-network gossip.

About the next step.

"I want to build a healing house," I said.

He set down his quill. "A what?"

"A dedicated building for treating the sick and injured. Separate from the maester's quarters, larger, with space for multiple patients. Clean rooms: boiled linens, washed walls, ventilation. A place where the protocols we've developed can be applied consistently, by trained staff, with proper records."

"You're describing something that doesn't exist."

"Not yet."

"The Faith operates houses for the sick. The silent sisters..."

"The Faith operates dying houses. Places where the terminally ill go to receive comfort and prayer. I'm talking about a place where people go to get better. Actively treated, with proven methods, by people trained in those methods."

The distinction mattered. The medieval world had something like hospitals: the Faith's houses of healing, the Citadel's own infirmary, but they were triage centers at best, hospices at worst. The concept of a hospital as a place of active treatment, with standardized protocols and trained staff, was centuries away.

Unless someone built one now.

"The cost," Lomys said.

"My grandmother will fund it." I was nearly certain of this. Olenna understood investment, and a healing house that demonstrably reduced mortality among Tyrell subjects was an investment in loyalty, productivity, and reputation.

"The staffing."

"We train people. The same way we trained the kitchen staff: protocols, practice, supervision. We don't need maesters. We need people who can follow procedures consistently."

"The Citadel may object to non-maesters practicing healing arts."

"The Citadel can object from Oldtown. We're in Highgarden, and the last time I checked, House Tyrell governs the Reach, not the Archmaesters."

That was bolder than I usually was with Lomys. But the vision was taking shape in my mind, solid and irresistible, and I could feel the next phase of the plan clicking into place with the precision of gears in a mechanism.

Sanitation saved individual lives. A healing house could save hundreds. And a network of healing houses — one in every major town in the Reach — could transform public health across the entire region.

But first, one. Here. Proof of concept.

"Write up a proposal," Lomys said. "I'll review it."

Coming from Lomys, that was practically a standing ovation.

I was already writing it in my head as I walked back to the workshop. Ward layout. Ventilation design. Water supply (boiled, obviously, but the logistics of supplying boiled water to a building housing twenty patients needed working out). Staff training protocols. Record-keeping systems. Quarantine procedures for contagious cases.

And the item I'd left off the list because writing it down made it real: a candid estimate of how loudly the Citadel would object once a Tyrell house of healing began outperforming maesters at the work the maesters claimed as theirs. Lomys had warned me once, in passing, and Lomys did not warn casually. Healing was their domain. Setting up a rival institution under our colors, run on principles they hadn't taught and couldn't yet refute, was a slow theft of authority. They had long memories. They would notice. By the letter Lomys was about to write to Ryam, they were already starting to.

I would have to plan for that. The healing house first, the politics after.

If handwashing could save a child's life, what could an entire institution built on the principle of cleanliness accomplish?

I intended to find out.

[CENTER][B]-- Olenna --[/B][/CENTER]

She had been expecting him. Mina had carried the shape of the maester's-study conversation up to her solar within a day, and Eda's mutterings over the bread oven had filled in the rest by the morning after. By the time Lomys was ready to bring the proposal in person, Olenna had known the substance of it for the better part of a week. The boy never asked for money directly when there was a respected intermediary who could ask in his place, and she had used the days to think.

Eight days after the maester's-study conversation, Lomys arrived in her solar with a sheaf of foolscap, an apologetic bow, and the demeanour of a man who has been asked to perform a sum and is not certain he likes the result.

"My lady. His lordship has prepared a proposal." Lomys set the foolscap on her writing-table. "He asked that I bring it to you with my own assessment of the costs, since the costs are the part on which my opinion was solicited rather than offered."

"Of course."

"It will be expensive."

"Of course."

She read.

Twenty beds in two wards, partitioned. Stone walls, slate roof to resist fire and vermin. A separate boil-house for water and linen. Privies sited downwind of the wells. Glazed windows in every room, which would by themselves eat a quarter of the timber budget. Six full-time attendants, plus a rotation of laundresses and scribes. Stipends for two healers, neither of them a chained maester. A monthly soap allotment from the Tyrell still-house lye-pots. A scriptorium corner for Lomys's records and the indexed ledger that would track every patient through every protocol.

The total at the bottom was close to nine hundred gold dragons in the first year, with a recurring annual cost of perhaps three hundred and fifty.

She read it twice. Once for the boy's plan, which was good. Once for the columns the boy had not realised he was writing.

The boy's instincts were sound and his arithmetic was wrong. Every wound that healed clean in a Highgarden ward was a piece of practice. Every protocol Lomys documented was an asset that could be transcribed, refined, rebranded, and sold under a different colour. A second house in Goldengrove. A third on Hightower lands. A fourth, eventually, in Oldtown itself, charging coin for the gouty knees of great lords from every kingdom that touched the Roseroad at a rate that paid for three more wards on Tyrell soil. Same techniques. Three prices. Four ledgers.

Stone walls and slate roof was an edifice in the geography of the Reach, visible from the road, fixed in the head of every passing bannerman and copied into the gossip of every village it lay near. The boil-house and the soap allotment were a supply chain that ran back to her own still-house and the apothecary network already feeding off it. The scriptorium corner was an asset library that would, in a year or two, contain the only documented set of clean-healing protocols in the Seven Kingdoms; she did not yet know what she would do with it, but she knew the shape of the thing one held when one held the only documented set of anything. The two unchained healers were the answer to the Citadel question, which was that one need not ask the Citadel.

The smallfolk would live, which was useful in itself and pleasing to the boy, who liked them. The technique would be honed on bodies the Citadel did not bother to count. By the time it reached the bodies the Citadel did count, it would already be a Tyrell property.

She filed the thought away the way she filed everything. The quiet word with Dalla Crane about apothecary licences had been arranged days ago, before the boy had ever set quill to foolscap.

The boy was building infrastructure and calling it a hospital.

Lomys watched her read. He would be expecting her to baulk, or to negotiate, or to refer the cost to Mace, who would refer it back to her in the slow circular way the Tyrell purse moved when someone other than Olenna was nominally in charge of it.

She did none of those things.

She drew a line under the boy's total and wrote a second number beneath it.

Lomys, without turning his head, read the new figure upside down. His brow rose by a careful degree. "That is more than twice his estimate, my lady."

"It is. And insufficient."

"My lady?"

"His estimate funds one healing house. I am funding the first of four." She set the quill down. "Add a clean wing on the east side. Three rooms, four beds, slate floor, lead glazing, the kind of bedding a high lord can rest his complaints upon. A separate ledger for that wing, and a separate purse. It pays its own keep at three times the cost of treating it, and the surplus crosses the corridor to feed the wards on this side. The boy will think we are hedging. We are paying for the second house and the third by the time the lordlings of the Reach realise that being seen at our healing house is a fashion that costs less than the gout it cures."

"And the records, my lady?"

"Are the most valuable thing in the building. Copies for him, copies for you, originals to me. Every protocol that works on a smallfolk fever in this ward can be retitled, recopied, and walked to Goldengrove with a different physician's name on the manuscript. My grandson will write a treatise eventually. It will be a long treatise. The four houses I intend to build will be operating before the first chapter of his treatise reaches a printer."

"He may notice."

"He will notice. He is not stupid, only kind, and he can be both at once for some years yet." She picked up her wine again. "When he notices, he and I will have a conversation. It will not be unpleasant. He will make the moral argument. I will make the argument about the House and the Reach: that the wards saving the children of Highgarden are paid for by the same lordlings whose gout we are also curing, and that the order matters. The boy wants to improve the realm. So do I. The realm is improved by the House that can afford to improve it, and a House that gives its medicine away cannot afford very much for very long. We will arrive somewhere in the middle, which will be exactly where I wanted to arrive in the first place. This is how the boy and I work."

Lomys did not quite smile.

"You disapprove."

"I keep my own counsel on these matters, my lady."

"That is also how you and I work. Send the proposal back to him with your endorsement. Add nothing about my notes. He will discover them in due course."

The maester bowed and went.

Olenna refilled her wine, looked out toward the south wall where her grandson was almost certainly already drafting the protocols for ward two, and made a small note in her own ledger about the writ she would need from Mace before the lemon harvest.
